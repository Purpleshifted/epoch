The Anthropocene, the golden spike, and what stands behind the rules of this work

Homo inventus (발견된 인류, “discovered humanity”) begins where a proposal was voted down: in March 2024 the Anthropocene was refused as a formal geological epoch. This page tells that story and shows which literature the work's rules — concrete, paths, pipes, vegetation, strata, names and the record — lean on, as encyclopedia entries. Anthropocene인류세

A proposed name for a geological epoch in which human activity has changed the Earth system enough to be recorded in its strata. Greek anthropos (human) plus -cene, the suffix of the Cenozoic epochs. The atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen and the ecologist Eugene Stoermer proposed it in the IGBP newsletter in 2000; Crutzen's one-page “Geology of mankind” in Nature (2002) made it widely known. The first proposed start was the late eighteenth century (the steam engine); the working group later settled on the “Great Acceleration” of the 1950s.

The golden spike · GSSP

Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point: the single reference section of rock, and the point in it, that defines the base of a geological time unit. The practice of marking the spot with a brass or gold-coloured spike gave it the name. The boundary must be a marker that can be recognised at the same moment in strata all over the planet — an isotope ratio, the first appearance of a fossil, fallout. The golden spike of our own epoch, the Holocene, sits in the NGRIP ice core from Greenland, at the abrupt warming 11,700 years ago (Walker et al. 2009).

Crawford Lake

A small lake near Milton, Ontario. It is meromictic — its deep water never mixes with the surface — so one layer of sediment (a varve) settles on the bottom every year, and time can be read year by year. The working group's boundary is the 1952 varve: plutonium (²³⁹⁺²⁴⁰Pu) from the first hydrogen-bomb test (Ivy Mike, 1 November 1952) rises sharply in that layer, together with spheroidal carbonaceous particles from fossil-fuel burning and a shift in nitrogen isotopes. Freeze cores taken in April 2023 were analysed varve by varve, and the boundary was fixed at the base of the organic lamina of 1952 (McCarthy et al. 2025).

The decision of March 2024THE VOTE

In the SQS vote that closed on 4 March 2024 the Anthropocene proposal was rejected, 12 against, 4 in favour, 2 abstentions (some reports say three). Passing needed 60 % in favour. Jan Zalasiewicz, chair of both the subcommission and the working group, and vice-chair Martin Head alleged breaches of procedure and asked for a review; on 20 March the IUGS stated that the vote was valid and the rejection final. By press accounts, a rejected proposal cannot be resubmitted for ten years.

The reasons given against (summarising members quoted in the press): the history of human change to the Earth is far longer — agriculture, the industrial revolution — and began at different times in different places (it is diachronous), so a single line at 1952 cuts that history wrongly; and seventy years, shorter than a human life, sits awkwardly as a unit of geological time.

The IUGS statement's conclusion: the term will nevertheless go on being used by scientists, politicians, economists and the public, and will remain “an invaluable descriptor of human impact on the Earth system”.

“We are in the Anthropocene, irrespective of a line on the time scale.”Francine McCarthy (Brock University, lead of the Crawford Lake research), as reported by The New York Times, 5 March 2024

The work starts here. Humanity's traces are everywhere, and yet they could not be pinned to a single point of matter. That gap, between the concept and its record, is where this work goes in.

The Anthropocene as an event

In 2022 Gibbard and ten co-authors published “The Anthropocene as an Event, not an Epoch” in the Journal of Quaternary Science, proposing that instead of a boundary at one date, the Anthropocene be treated as a geological event unfolding over millennia, differently in different places. After the rejection the IUGS also pointed to this framing. The work takes no side in the dispute; it uses the fact that the concept did not become an official record.

Hyperobjects

The philosopher Timothy Morton's term, from his book of the same name (2013): things massively distributed in time and space relative to humans — global warming, Styrofoam, plutonium with its half-life of tens of thousands of years. He names five qualities: viscous, nonlocal, temporally undulating, phased, interobjective. We only ever meet their local manifestations; no one has seen the whole. This is why the work gives its visitors no overview.

Trace fossils · technofossils

A trace fossil (ichnofossil) preserves not an organism's body but its activity — footprints and trackways, burrows and borings, nests, coprolites. It stands against the body fossil (bones, shells); the field that studies it is ichnology. The idea that the unit of the record is the act, not the subject, comes from here. A technofossil, a term proposed by Zalasiewicz et al. (2014), is the fossil that human-made things and structures — concrete, plastics, pipes, cities — will leave in future strata. The work looks for humanity's trace fossils in the dwellings, waterways and paths of the city.

Sound as a web project웹사운드 — THE ENGINE, NOISECRAFT, MAGENTA

The sound is made in the browser, by the browser. No audio files, no DAW, no plug-ins: the headset in the personal view and the loudspeakers of the room each run a Web Audio engine fed by the same event log, a NoiseCraft patch runs as a second instrument inside a hidden page, and Google's Magenta models are wired in as an optional source of variation.

One event log, two scales. Boxes are modules of apps/web/src/lib/sound; the dashed box is the NoiseCraft page; grey boxes are optional or off for the show.

The engine — what one person hears

Every half second the personal view folds the event log and reads a handful of signals: where I am, how fast I walk, whether I have stayed within 1.5 units for six seconds, who is within earshot, the strength of the path underfoot, how close the nearest concrete is, how much grass surrounds me. The ground carries a harmony field: each band of ground has a key and a mode, and from them a seventh chord, the place chord. A single number, lock = max(path, stay × company), says how strongly the place pulls my notes onto that chord.

A name is a motif — a short contour on an eight-step score, drawn once per life and shown as a glyph. The sequencer plans one bar at a time on a bar clock that every voice shares: my motif realised on the key, snapped to chord tones by lock; a neighbour's motif as a second voice; call and answer when two people stay together (even bars mine, odd bars theirs); a bass line on the paths; a pad when a building is being born. The bar length itself is decided by interaction — on a path or while building together the bar tightens by a few percent.

The engine schedules each note as two Web Audio nodes (an oscillator and a gain) a little ahead of time. Other people's voices pass through a PannerNode with the browser's built-in HRTF, so they sit in front, behind, above — before they are ever seen. Three rooms are crossfaded by convolution: open ground (short, dark), concrete (bright, early reflections), a hall when people build together. A low-pass filter dulls everything near concrete; grass adds a thin band of noise. A limiter sits after the master, and the output is routed to the headset device with setSinkId so the room's speakers and the visitor's headset can share one machine.

The whole — what the room hears

The loudspeakers take the same log and the same motifs and fold them at the scale of the whole: every motif of the last ten minutes plays two octaves down as a sawtooth, through one low-pass filter that opens with the share of people walking, a hall and soft saturation, into one mass; a noise floor grows with the number of people; each person's rhythm cell ticks as a short noise burst. There is no interaction here at all. The same event that was a musical moment for one person is one cell of a structure for the whole — and, on the wall, one slab of concrete.

NoiseCraft — the artist's instrument as a static machine

NoiseCraft is an open-source visual programming language for sound synthesis by Maxime Chevalier-Boisvert, in the family of Pure Data and Max/MSP: one data type (floating-point numbers), no distinction between control and audio signals, compiled to an AudioWorklet and run in the browser. The work forks it and serves its editor from the app. The artist's patch nc_noise_patch — three random chains with a BPM clock, delays, reverb and LFO-driven filters, carried over from the artist's earlier project umwelt — plays in a hidden page and keeps its own output. The code turns none of its knobs. Only two things go in: the music clock (the patch's sequencer clock and its BPM knob both receive the bar length decided outside) and harmonic sequences (sixteen steps a bar of pitch and gate for each receiving node — a root voice, a colour voice, a fifth, the pad's upper four tones). The patch decides for itself how often each voice sounds — some envelopes follow our gates, others are fired by the patch's own random triggers and take only the pitch. Phase flows the other way: the patch's clock-out pulses are counted, and the engine's grid is anchored to them. On a bench page the artist can register a knob by clicking it in the editor and say in words what it should respond to; the mapping is then written as code, never as a UI.

Magenta — variation by models (wired, switched off)

Two models from Google's Magenta.js are ported from umwelt and loaded lazily after the first gesture (TensorFlow.js and the checkpoints come from Google's servers, so they need the network): MusicVAE (mel_2bar_small) interpolates my next four bars with a neighbour's in latent space, the point on the line chosen by how long and how close we have been; MusicRNN (chord_pitches_improv) continues my phrase for 32 steps over the chords of the ground once lock has held for two bars. A phrase is 32 sixteenth steps of MIDI; after a model's phrase the voice rests two bars and returns to its motif. For the November show the models are off: a procedural blend — swapping degrees of the two motifs, keeping my register — stands in, and whenever a model fails the engine keeps that blend. The architecture leaves the door open.

Why this counts as a web-sound project

The browser is the only instrument. Everything a visitor hears is synthesised by the device in their hands, from the same log that builds the ground, with nothing sent from a server. The music is a pure function of the log and the bar number: fold the same events again and the same bar sounds the same. Time is musical time, decided by what people do — bars tighten on paths and when building together — and it is not the world's clock. Spatial hearing comes from the browser's own HRTF; rooms from generated impulse responses; the exhibition's routing of headset and speakers from setSinkId and a compressor per engine. Each note costs two nodes, voices are budgeted, chains are capped and a watchdog reads the audio clock's speed, so an eight-gigabyte Mac can run the wall, the headset and the bots at once. A visual synth and two machine-learned models plug into the same bar clock as modules. The score is a living document, docs/sound-logic.md, rewritten with the artist's ear after every listening session.
